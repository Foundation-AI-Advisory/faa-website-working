Reading the Invoice Was Never the Hard Part

By Benjamin DeMichael · October 24, 2026

An invoice can be read perfectly and still get stuck. Fix the ownership and evidence behind price mismatches, missing receipts, and approvals.

In this article

An invoice arrives. The system reads the supplier, invoice number, amount, and due date correctly.

Then it stops.

There is no purchase order. The price differs from the quote. Receiving has not confirmed the delivery. The person who can explain the charge is out. Accounting sends another email and waits.

That is the operating problem I would examine before buying more invoice automation. Perfect document extraction does not tell the business whether it should approve a payment.

Look at the queue after extraction

Have the accounts payable lead review a recent group of invoices that required manual intervention. Record why each one stopped, who had the information needed to resolve it, and how long resolution took.

Keep the categories specific enough to act on. A missing receipt, a disputed quantity, a duplicate invoice, and an unknown approver are different problems. Labeling all of them “manual processing” hides the work.

Also distinguish handling time from waiting time. An invoice may take five minutes of accounting effort and sit unresolved for several days. Reducing the five minutes does not eliminate the delay.

The objective should be shorter resolution time, fewer duplicate or incorrect payments, and more reliable visibility into cash commitments.

Establish the records the decision depends on

Begin with Data Curation & Governance. Vendor identities, invoice references, purchase orders, receipts, approved prices, and payment terms need consistent definitions and traceable sources. Someone must own corrections when they do not agree.

Keep vendor master changes and payment-detail verification under established controls. An emailed document should not, by itself, authorize a change to where money is sent. Finance must define the checks and separation of responsibilities before automating the workflow.

A confidence score from an extraction tool is about what it believes it read. It is not approval that the goods were received, the price was agreed, or the obligation is valid.

Give every exception a route

Workflow Optimization should turn a stalled invoice into an assigned piece of work. Define the owner for each exception, the evidence needed to resolve it, a response expectation, and an escalation path.

Purchasing may own a price mismatch. Receiving may own missing delivery confirmation. A department manager may own an unapproved service charge. Accounts payable should be able to see the status without restarting the conversation each time.

Keep the resolution attached to the invoice record. A decision buried in someone’s inbox is difficult to reuse when the same issue returns.

Automate inside the approved boundaries

Once the data and exception paths are dependable, AI can help extract fields, propose coding, summarize discrepancies, and draft requests for missing information. Preserve the source document and make corrections visible.

The controller should set the approval boundaries and required checks. An uncertain result, a duplicate concern, or a material disagreement should enter the review queue. AI should not infer permission to pay from a plausible explanation.

For the first 30 days, have the AP lead and controller focus on the two most common exception types. Establish the current volume, age, handling time, and resolution owner. Fix those paths, then compare results using the same definitions.

The expected business impact is lower cycle time and risk exposure, with better cash-flow planning. Any broader implementation should have an agreed economic case, a production support owner, and a way to stop or correct processing when controls fail.

When the organization knows how to resolve an invoice, automation can help it move. If nobody owns the exception, the technology simply gets the document to the waiting room faster.

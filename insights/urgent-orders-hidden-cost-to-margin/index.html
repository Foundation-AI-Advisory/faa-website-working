The Rush Order Is Costing More Than You Think

By Benjamin DeMichael · October 24, 2026

A rush order changes more than its own delivery date. Make setup time, displaced work, overtime, and approval ownership visible before saying yes.

Sales calls it an opportunity. Production calls it another schedule change. The customer calls it urgent.

All three may be right. The problem starts when nobody owns the full cost of saying yes.

A rush order can be worth accepting. It can protect an important customer or fill a genuine opening in capacity. But its selling price does not tell you whether it is good business. You also need to understand the work it displaces.

Follow the disruption beyond the order

Start with the last several orders that jumped the queue. Ask the scheduler what changed after each one was accepted.

A machine may have been torn down early. A planned run may have been split into two setups. Materials may have arrived on premium freight. A supervisor may have moved experienced people away from another job. A customer whose order was already promised may now be waiting.

Those consequences often land in different places. Freight is visible in purchasing. Overtime is visible in payroll. Lost production time is harder to see. Customer service absorbs the calls.

If the review stops at the rush order’s quoted margin, leadership can miss the cost carried by the rest of the schedule.

Make the decision visible before accepting it

The operations leader and commercial leader should agree on an exception process together. Sales needs a timely answer. Operations needs enough information to make a credible commitment.

Define what makes an order urgent, who can approve a schedule change, and what information must accompany the request. Include the requested date, material availability, constrained work center, likely displacement, and any additional customer charge.

There should be a named decision maker when the tradeoff crosses departments. Otherwise, the person answering the phone can end up making a capacity decision for the entire business.

Keep room for judgment. A strategic customer may justify accepting a lower return on one order. Record that decision so an intentional exception does not become an invisible operating habit.

Stabilize the inputs before applying AI

Start with Data Curation & Governance: reliable order dates, routings, setup assumptions, material status, capacity calendars, and reason codes for changes.

Then improve the workflow: a short approval path, a consistent cutoff for schedule changes, and a record of who accepted the consequence. Ask whether the same urgency could have been prevented through an earlier customer conversation or better material planning.

Only then consider AI assistance. It can help summarize the request, surface relevant constraints, and prepare alternatives for the scheduler. Any proposed impact needs validation against the scheduling rules and current operating data. AI should not silently reprioritize committed customer work.

Measure the pattern for 30 days

The operations leader should own a 30-day review with sales and finance. Start this week by logging every accepted rush request, its reason, approval, and affected orders. Use existing records before commissioning a new application.

Review setup hours, overtime, premium freight, on-time delivery for displaced orders, and the contribution expected from the accepted work. Avoid counting the same cost twice. Compare the result with the actual decision available at the time, not an ideal schedule that never existed.

The objective is better margin and throughput with fewer preventable disruptions. Finance should validate the benefit before funding an AI implementation; operations should own the result after launch.

The right question is not whether the business can squeeze in one more order. It is whether the people saying yes understand what that answer commits the business to do.

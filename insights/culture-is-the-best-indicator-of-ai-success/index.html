AI Design & Implementation Article 03 — AI Design & Implementation

Culture Is the Best Indicator of Whether AI Will Succeed

By Benjamin DeMichael · October 23, 2026

How a business handles accountability, uncomfortable information, and change tells you a lot about what will happen when AI enters the organization.

In this article

If I had to pick one indicator of how successful an organization will be with AI, I would pick its culture.

I would look at how the business handles problems today. Can people challenge a process? Do departments share information? Does leadership follow through? When something goes wrong, does someone take ownership?

Those behaviors tell you a lot about what will happen when AI enters the business.

A company can afford the technology and still struggle to get value from it. The harder question is whether the organization is willing to change how it works.

Culture shows up in everyday operating decisions.

People often talk about culture as values, engagement, or whether employees enjoy coming to work. Those things matter. But for AI implementation, I’m interested in some very practical questions.

Can someone tell their boss the data is wrong?

Can an estimator explain that the documented process has very little to do with how estimates actually get built?

Can accounting question information coming from operations without starting a fight?

Will leadership give people time to improve the work, or expect them to do it on top of an already full day?

The answers affect whether a problem gets corrected or carried into the next system.

AI can make that existing problem move much faster.

You cannot improve work that people are afraid to explain honestly.

Consider a construction company that wants to use AI to prepare estimates faster.

The software might help extract quantities, organize scope, and assemble a draft. But someone still has to explain which historical labor hours are reliable, which jobs lost money, and where experienced estimators routinely override the standard assumptions.

That knowledge lives with people.

If those people believe the project threatens their jobs, discounts their experience, or will be used to blame them for past mistakes, why would they openly share everything they know?

Calling that “resistance to change” misses the point. Leadership has to explain the objective, involve the people doing the work, and be honest about what may change.

Their participation is part of building a system the business can trust.

Leadership has to participate in the change it expects.

If the president ignores the agreed process, everyone notices.

If managers continue to reward individual workarounds while asking for consistent data, the workarounds will continue.

If the initiative belongs to IT but nobody in operations owns the result, there is a good chance the business gets a functioning tool without a meaningful improvement.

The leader responsible for the business outcome needs to stay accountable for it. IT has a critical role, but margin, throughput, cash flow, and customer delivery already have business owners. AI does not remove that responsibility.

The foundation still comes first.

At Foundation AI Advisory, our implementation sequence is deliberate:

  1. Data Curation & Governance.
  2. Workflow Optimization.
  3. AI Design & Implementation.

Culture affects whether each step holds up.

Someone has to maintain the data after it is cleaned. People have to agree on how work should move between departments. Managers have to address exceptions. Employees need to be able to question an AI output without being treated as obstacles to progress.

A culture that values accountability and honest feedback gives that work a chance to stick.

Start with one business problem and make the commitment visible.

For the first 30 days, have the operating leader responsible for that problem bring together the people who do the work. Establish the current performance, identify unreliable data, and agree on the changes required.

For estimating, that might mean measuring turnaround time, rework, and estimate-to-actual labor variance. Assign one accountable owner and define the improvement that would justify moving into production.

That gives the team a business objective, a way to measure progress, and a reason to participate.

Installing software is one milestone. Getting people to trust it, use it appropriately, and improve business performance is the work.

Before asking how ready your company is for AI, ask how well it handles accountability, uncomfortable information, and change today.

That is where I would look for the answer.
